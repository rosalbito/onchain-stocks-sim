Arbitrum replaced Timeboost with priority gas auctions and a paid Fast Feed in September 2026: inside each 125 ms round, the highest fee goes first, ties to whoever reached the sequencer first. Robinhood Chain is first come, first served today; CoinDesk reported, from one anonymous source, that it is considering PGA, so say “reportedly”. Two knobs set the bids. α (alpha) is each sniper’s priority fee as a share of the prize it sees; β (beta) is the LP’s fee on its cancel as a share of what it could lose on that quote. Both are our choices, not from research. In our model α = 0.1: the snipers together bid 30% of the prize, split equally, 10% each with three snipers. That is the most they can bid and not lose money on average, which is where competition between bidders pushes it, so it is the generous case for the chain. Fees are paid whether the order wins or not, like gas. The LP’s cancel bids nothing, β = 0, so it loses almost every race: 0.5% won against 32% under first come, first served. Follow the money for one weekday hour: the LP loses $40.48 to snipers, twice what it loses under first come, first served, because a bid outranks even a cancel that arrived first. Of that, about $26 goes to the venue as the 1 bp taker fee on the snipers’ many small fills, $14 goes to the chain as priority fees, and the snipers keep about 40 cents. So PGA makes the sniping roughly break even, but the LP still pays, and the chain and the venue collect. And bidding does not save the LP. We swept β = 0, 0.25, 0.5 and 1. At β = 1, its whole exposure on each cancel, it wins 25% of races, but its fees bring its total cost to $41.84, more than the $40.48 it loses by not bidding. The best is β = 0.5, $34.96 on a weekday, about 14% less: bidding trims the loss, it doesn’t stop it. At the Monday open every β > 0 costs the LP more than not bidding: $23.41 to $34.05 against $20.19.
